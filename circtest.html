Question 1 of 5

Passage 1 (Questions 1‑5)

Oxygen is carried in the blood primarily by red blood cells (erythrocytes), which contain Hemoglobin — a tetrameric protein composed of four polypeptide chains, each with a heme group binding one O₂ molecule. Hemoglobin’s binding of O₂ is cooperative: when one O₂ binds, affinity for the others increases, giving a sigmoidal dissociation curve.

In the tissues where CO₂ and H⁺ concentrations are high (lower pH), hemoglobin’s affinity for O₂ decreases (the Bohr effect), enabling O₂ release; in the lungs where pH is higher and CO₂ lower, uptake is favoured. Myoglobin, found in muscle, has one heme and a hyperbolic dissociation curve, serving as an O₂ reservoir. Fetal hemoglobin (Hb F) binds O₂ more tightly than adult hemoglobin, because it binds 2,3-BPG less well.

In Sickle cell anemia a single amino-acid substitution (Glu → Val) in the β-chain of hemoglobin causes structural changes that impair O₂ transport. Blood is considered a connective tissue, and mature erythrocytes lack a nucleus, mitochondria and other organelles, maximizing space for hemoglobin. Leukocytes are classified into granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) and agranulocytes; platelets are fragments that initiate clot formation.

Deoxygenated blood returns to the heart via the venae cavae, then is pumped to the lungs for oxygenation, and arteries carry oxygenated blood away from the heart while veins return blood toward the heart. Cardiac output equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume, and systole is the contraction phase during which blood is ejected. The hormone ADH increases water reabsorption; ANP lowers blood pressure by increasing sodium and water excretion.
